Menswear arie Antoinette built a working farm at Ver- sailles to indulge in an idealised vision of rural life without the toil. Today, stylish men are embracing a sim- ilar fantasy as they adopt the practical clothes worn by British farmers in ever more refined and luxurious forms. At the Milan menswear shows last month, GQ global editorial director Will Welch was striking in a roomy waxed jacket over a shirt and tie, giving him the air of a young Prince Philip — patrician yet practical. Elsewhere at the shows, Beka Gvishiani, the man behind influ- ential fashion news Instagram account @stylenotcom, layered an oversized, forest-green waxed field coat from Miu Miu with blue jeans and a pair of splay- toed Loewe loafers. He looked ready for a weekend at Soho Farmhouse, the members’ club in rural Oxfordshire, central England. High-end designers are offering their own takes on British countryside attire. Miuccia Prada has made Miu Miu arare success story in the beleaguered luxury market by selling the aforementioned logo-clad waxed jackets, whose gener- ous, raglan-sleeve cuts and leather collars offer a cooler, more contempo- rary twist on the traditional Barbour (£3,650, mytheresa.com), The brand also sells countryside-ready Harrington jackets, while Auralee and The Row have included canvas field jackets in their spring menswear collections. “The trend for ‘country pursuit’ out- fits is clear in our sales this season,” says Sophie Jordan, menswear buying direc- tor of online department store MyTheresa. “Waxed jackets from brands like Miu Miu have performed strongly, as have Prada’s quilted jackets. Even check shirting, seen at Tom Ford and God’s True Cashmere, nods to the trend in amore everyday way.” The high street is following suit. A recent visit to Zara’s Regent Street store in London revealed racks of roomy waxed jackets in scarlet, navy and earth tones, all finished with corduroy or faux-suede collars. Such pieces wouldn't have looked out of place in a country- side outfitters such as Cordings or Bar- bour nearby, yet there they were, being snapped up by city-dwelling trend- junkies itching for a fast-fashion hit. One theory behind the revival of Brit- ish outdoor gear is that it serves as a sar- torial escape from our growing +" ao disconnect with the natural world —a way to dress for the wilderness, as it becomes ever more elusive. More likely, though, is that it indulges a desire to dress like the landed gentry, and projects an image of wealth. (Could Guy Ritchie’s 2024 hit Netflix series The Gentlemen be in part to thank? I think so.) For Esquire style director Johnny Davis, it also reflects a response to the increasingly unpredictable sociopolitical climate. “The current fetishisation of British outdoor wear is an evolution of gorpcore, cottagecore and the ‘coastal grandpa’ aesthetic,” says Davis. “When the world feels uncertain and chaotic, we romanticise tradition, reliability and authenticity — the Above: Theo James as Eddie Horniman in “The Gentlemen’ Right: Holland & Holland Northwood tweed field coat, £995, hollandandholland.com' antithesis of fast fashion and fleeting trends. And just as the countryside has always been a refuge for those burnt out by city life, from Withnail and I to Soho Farm- house, so too are the clothes designed to be worn there.” Are these fashion-led interpretations of coun- try staples superior to the originals made by mid-level heritage players such as Barbour, Hunter and Lavenham? Such brands have long produced high-quality waxed jackets, checked shirts and wellies at fair prices, and for party planner Henry Conway, they will never be usurped. “Real coun- try folk love British outdoor brands for a reason: the clothes last,” he says. “The pieces are workhorses that get the job done.” Few garments embody that better than the Barbour jacket. Founded in South Shields, north-east England, in 1894, Barbour has spent over a century perfecting its waxed jackets, earning multiple royal warrants — Queen Eliza- beth II was a life-long fan. The recognis- able Beaufort jacket, designed by Lady Margaret Barbour in the early 1980s, has been worn by everyone from King Charles III to David Beckham. Starting at £299, it’s a piece of outdoor heritage at a reasonable price. “Most heritage brands start by answering a need,” says Ian Bergin, Bar- bour’s director of menswear. “Ours was providing durable, intelligently designed outerwear for the working people of the North East — miners, fish- ermen, shipbuilders and farmers.” When it comes to men’s fashion, function sells — and few things are as reliably functional as classic British rural outdoor wear. By Teo van den Broeke Lavenham also offers countryside- ready staples that blend style and func- tion. Favoured by both Queen Elizabeth II and Princess Anne, the company, based in Suffolk, eastern England, cre- ated the first nylon horse blanket in 1969 and its collarless quilted liner jack- ets have a contemporary, Japanese- inspired aesthetic. Great British outdoors gear extends well beyond weatherproof coats. For wellies, Hunter is the classic choice, but for a pair youre less likely to spot some- one else wearing, head to Le Chameau or Dubarry. Fairfax & Favor reigns in Chelsea boots, while Sch6ffel and Darzi lead in gilets. For cords and velvets, Oliver Brown, Sirplus and Favourbrook are go-tos, while quality tweed is best found at House of Bruar or Purdey. Hol- land & Holland also offers an array of elegant tweed field jackets, which would look equally at home draped over a bar stool at The George in London as they would stomping across the grouse moor. Holland & Holland’s outerwear is so good, in fact, that I’m seriously consid- ering a Northwood tweed jacket in Haw- thorn for my own London Fashion Week look this weekend (£995, hollandand- holland.com).
